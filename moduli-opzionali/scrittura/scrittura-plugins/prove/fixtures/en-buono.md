# Tunguska

At about 7:17 a.m. on June 30, 1908, a bluish light crossed the sky above central Siberia. Farmers and herders in the Podkamennaya Tunguska basin saw it. A few minutes later came a flash, then a sound that one witness compared to a cannon shot.

The blast flattened forest over roughly 2,000 square kilometers. Trees lay in a radial pattern, their trunks pointing away from the center. Seismic stations in Europe recorded the shock, and in London people reportedly read newspapers outdoors at midnight under a glowing sky.

Nobody went to look for years. Leonid Kulik, a mineralogist, reached the area in 1927 expecting a crater and a buried meteorite. He found neither. The trees at the center still stood, stripped of their branches, while those farther out lay flat. That detail points to an explosion in the air rather than an impact on the ground.

What exploded is still debated. Most researchers favor a stony asteroid or a fragment of a comet that broke apart between five and ten kilometers up. Estimates of the energy range from a few to a few dozen megatons of TNT, and the figure depends on assumptions about altitude and angle. No fragment has been found that all agree on, so the question stays open.
